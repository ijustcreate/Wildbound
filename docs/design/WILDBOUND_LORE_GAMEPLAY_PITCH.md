# Wildbound — the board, the game, and the world inside it

**Status:** concept and planning only. This document is not canon and does not authorize implementation. It reframes the pitch around the intended Jumanji-like premise: people gather to play a strange board game, and the game's events become real. It also develops the separate reality and lore of Wildbound itself.

## The hook

Friends gather around an old board game called **Wildbound**. The rules say to roll, move your token, and read the event. The group expects a game night. Then the event happens in the room.

A flock bursts through the windows. Something is scratching beneath the floorboards. A storm forms indoors. A creature from a painted card steps into the lamplight. The players have to survive the event, protect one another, and figure out how to finish the round. The board does not merely describe an adventure: **it makes the adventure happen**.

In **Wildbound mode**, the danger changes scale. The board pulls the players through its surface and into Wildbound, the world the game came from. The party must travel its strange biomes, survive the events there, and find the way back to the board—and home.

**The game is the portal. The roll is the trigger. The event is the encounter. The group is in it together.**

## Two ways to play the same game

| Mode | Where the players are | What a board event does | Main fantasy |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Game Night** | In the house / real-world game space | Something from the event enters, transforms, or threatens that space. Players fend it off, outlast it, or satisfy the event's condition so the round can continue. | “We were just playing—now the game is happening to us.” |
| **Wildbound** | Inside the world represented by the board | The roll sends the party to a biome or location. The event is a real trial in that place; the party must survive and continue the journey. | “We have been pulled inside the game. How do we make it home?” |

These are two expressions of one premise, not unrelated campaigns. The same roll, event, creature, and rules can be experienced as a home incursion or as a challenge inside Wildbound. The house is the frame story and possible play space; it is not automatically one of the world's natural biomes. The current project also has a playable House environment, which can support the house-side fantasy.

## What is Wildbound?

Wildbound is a real place with its own geography, histories, peoples, creatures, and rules. It is not a dream, a set of disconnected levels, or a metaphor for the players' feelings. The board was made there—or made from something that came from there—and it preserves a dangerous connection to that world.

The world should feel older and larger than the game that represents it. The board contains only a partial, simplified route through Wildbound. Its spaces are not the whole map; they are recognizable thresholds, landmarks, and trouble spots. The event verses are fragments of Wildbound's history, warnings, boasts, and songs that the board has compressed into rules players can read aloud.

### A proposed origin for the board

Long ago, Wildbound's far-flung regions were connected by **Waystones**: carved markers that let travelers find old roads between biomes. As routes became unsafe, people made a portable map from a living boundary-tree—wood said to grow where one place touches another. The board was meant to help stranded travelers remember the crossings and call for help.

It was eventually carried out of Wildbound and turned into a game. Its maker simplified real warnings into playful verses and dice rules, believing the distance would make it harmless. The board retained the older binding: when a verse is read and the die lands, the named event answers. The rules are the lock and the key at once.

This origin is a proposal, not a fixed answer. It offers a practical reason for the board, dice, verses, tokens, and two modes to belong to one fiction. The final truth could be more mysterious, but it should preserve the central rule: **playing Wildbound activates a real connection to Wildbound**.

### Why events cross over

Wildbound is not invading Earth on purpose. The board's connection is unstable and literal. A roll can pull an event across the boundary into the house, or pull the players across the boundary into Wildbound. A creature that appears in the living room is not a random monster teleporting for no reason: it is answering the board's call, following a route, or completing the event the roll invoked.

This gives the fiction room for both comedy and danger. A stampede through a hallway can be chaotic and funny; it is still frightening when the players have to avoid being trampled. A harmless little creature might leave the room with something important. A terrible foe might be following a rule that can be understood and outplayed.

## The story engine

The first mystery is not “who is the dark lord?” It is:

1. Why does this board make its verses real?
2. Why are the events becoming more frequent, more dangerous, or less predictable?
3. What is happening to the routes and places inside Wildbound?
4. Can the players finish the game without leaving Wildbound trapped, or bringing more of it home?

The long story should connect answers to places and people in Wildbound. The board's trouble is a symptom of a real crisis there. The players' goal is not to conquer the world; it is to understand the rules, survive what the game summons, and decide what to do about the connection.

### The central conflict: the failing crossings

The Waystones that once linked Wildbound's regions are going dark, shifting, or waking at the wrong time. When a crossing fails, things meant to stay on one route spill onto another. Seasons overlap. A creature follows a trail that ends in the wrong biome. An old guardian appears where no one remembers calling it. Events printed on the board are increasingly out of date because Wildbound itself is changing.

This is a concrete world crisis, not a generic spreading evil. There can be antagonists—raiders, territorial creatures, an ambitious collector, a guardian that mistakes the players for a threat—but no single villain needs to have caused every problem. The crossings may be failing through age, misuse, or the board's repeated calls. Players can discover which explanations are true as they travel.

### The board's rules are story rules

- **A roll calls an event.** The event must begin promptly and visibly, in either mode.
- **An event has a condition.** Defeat or drive off a threat, protect a space, find a marked object, survive a timer, or reach the end of a route. “Kill everything” is one possible condition, not the default for every card.
- **The group can finish the event.** Completing its condition lets play advance, opens a route, or quiets the incursion. The board's rules should be learnable enough for players to plan around them.
- **The game has teeth.** Ignoring a roll, breaking the rules, or failing an event has a consequence. It should create a new problem, not abruptly end the story or erase a long save.
- **The board remembers.** Important events leave a mark: a new token, a rewritten verse, a damaged space, a saved route, or a changed relationship. The game-night board becomes the group's campaign artifact.

## The opening: a complete first-night story

### Prologue: “Just one round”

The party is in a lived-in house. The game is discovered in a closet, attic, estate box, or among a relative's belongings. No lore lecture is needed. The board has hand-painted spaces, carved tokens, a peculiar die, and a short rulebook with a few odd warnings. The group chooses explorers, picks a token, and starts playing.

The early turns are playful and legible. A harmless event proves the premise. The next roll sends a recognizable creature into the room. The party moves, dodges, protects a friend, and completes the card's condition. A final turn pulls the group into Wildbound, revealing that the house-side events were not isolated hauntings: the board has been opening a route from somewhere.

### Chapter I: “The First Crossing” — Heartwood

The party lands in a forest around an old Waystone. A path is marked on the board, but the real path has been overgrown and the marker is cracked. Local travelers recognize the board's symbols as warnings once used by people who knew this region. A creature from a recent game-night event is also here, confused and far from its usual habitat.

The party survives a forest event, meets a guide, and finds that the Waystone reacts to the board. The immediate objective is clear: reach a safe clearing and learn how the route works. The broader mystery is opened, not solved. The chapter ends when the party finds a route home—or reaches a deeper region on the board.

### Chapter II: “The Rules Are Older” — choose the next region

The board's verses refer to real places. Players travel to a new destination to learn what a verse means, why the crossing there failed, or where a missing token belongs. The order can vary. The dice still create surprises, while a campaign goal gives the group a reason to choose the next destination.

### Finale: “One Last Roll”

The party learns that the board can close the connection, repair it, or leave it open under new rules. The last event tests what they have learned, not only their combat strength. The ending should resolve the first crisis while leaving the world available for further adventures. Players decide whether the board returns to the house as a game, stays in Wildbound, or becomes a safer bridge between worlds.

## Wildbound's world: a distinct identity for each region

The biomes should not feel like a checklist of familiar fantasy environments. Each needs a visual signature, a local way of life, a kind of danger, and a reason a board event would be remembered there. Let a verse be a clue to the place, not just a rhyme about the next enemy.

| Region / environment in the project | What makes it Wildbound | Story material and event possibilities |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Forest / Heartwood** | Vast old trees, trails that predate current maps, clearings used as meeting places, wildlife with familiar but surprising habits. | A path appears only at dusk; a guardian mistakes the party for loggers; a herd crosses through the board's marked trail. |
| **Desert** | Heat, exposed ruins, moving sand, scarce water, and landmarks that vanish or reappear after wind. | A buried route, mirage that leads to something real, a water convoy, a creature that hunts by vibration. |
| **Ice / Frostlands** | Long night light, frozen crossings, buried structures, animals adapted to silence and cold. | A cracking lake, a missing warmth-stone, a whiteout separating the party, something waking beneath the ice. |
| **Jungle / Temple** | Dense life growing through built spaces; ruins still used by living inhabitants rather than empty treasure rooms. | A temple mechanism that redirects water, an interrupted migration, a guardian trial whose rules can be solved instead of brute-forced. |
| **Beach / Coast** | Tides expose and reclaim objects; islands, piers, and shallows make routes change over time. | A wreck that arrives in pieces, a tide race, shore creatures defending nesting grounds, a message carried by the surf. |
| **Graveyard / Cemetery** | A cared-for place of memory, with distinct customs for names, spirits, and the dead; eerie without making every inhabitant evil. | A missing name, a wake interrupted by a Reaper, a spirit that needs help finishing a journey, a grave marker that moved. |
| **Swamp / Bayou** | Water and land form a maze of islands and channels; sound, lanterns, and boardwalks matter as much as sight. | Lanterns moving in the reeds, a nesting bank under threat, a ferry route blocked, a sound that imitates a party member. |
| **Caverns / Hollowdeep** | Underground chambers have their own ecology, mineral light, water, and ceiling hazards; the cave is not simply a dark dungeon. | Singing geodes, a rockfall that divides the party, a blind creature following vibrations, a new passage opened by draining a pool. |
| **Shadow Realm** | A place shaped by strange light and silhouette, with its own native life; ominous does not automatically mean hostile. | Keeping several lights alive, a pale hart leading the party, hounds crossing a veil, a route that exists only while lit. |
| **House / urban play space** | A familiar human-scale place that the game makes unsafe and uncanny: hallways, rooms, furniture, doors, the board table, hiding places, and improvised defenses. | A stampede through the kitchen, an event changing room-to-room, protecting the board from a creature, using household objects to survive. |

### Roster note: what exists versus what is playable in the first campaign

The current environment list in `src/expansion.mjs` defines **ten entries**: Forest, Desert, Ice, House, Temple, Beach, Graveyard, Swamp, Caverns, and Shadow. That is **nine non-house destinations plus House**. “Temple” is the project's jungle/temple environment, not a separate jungle and temple count. The existing Wildbound travel campaign note describes **six destinations**: Forest, Ice, Desert, Jungle Temple, Beach, and Cemetery. So the code roster has nine non-house environment choices, while the known campaign route has six; “eight biomes plus house/urban” may reflect an intended or differently grouped roster. This pitch treats all nine as available worldbuilding material and leaves the final canon roster open for review.

The word “urban” needs a separate decision. The code's `house` environment supports the board-night setting; it does not by itself establish a city biome. If a true city or town region is desired, it could be a future Wildbound destination with neighborhoods, markets, roofs, and inhabitants—but it should not be counted as already present just because the house environment exists.

## Core gameplay loops

### Game Night loop: gather → roll → survive → finish the card → keep playing

1. **Gather:** choose explorers, decide who sits where / claims which token, check the short rules, and prepare a few objects or tools.
2. **Roll and read:** a player rolls; the board selects or reveals a verse/event. The room reacts immediately so the group understands that the game caused it.
3. **Survive together:** players move around the house space, protect one another, use weapons/tools/household improvisation, and respond to the event's objective. Some players may draw danger away while others complete the objective.
4. **Resolve the rule:** drive off, contain, outlast, rescue, or defeat the summoned threat; satisfy the event condition and stabilize the board. Avoid making a repeated kill-wave the only form of play.
5. **Take the consequence:** earn a clue, token, tool, or safe turn; see a board mark or house detail change. A failed event can leave a complication for the next roll.
6. **Choose whether to continue:** players can risk another turn, pause and regroup, or pursue a clue that points into Wildbound.

The house mode must feel like a full game loop in its own right, not just a tutorial room before the “real” adventure. Rooms and furniture should matter tactically; the board and event cards should make each round legible; short sessions should still have a beginning, escalation, and payoff.

### Wildbound mode loop: roll → cross over → survive the world → follow the board → return

1. **Set the expedition:** at the board, choose a destination or campaign clue, bring equipment, and agree on a shared goal.
2. **Cross over:** the roll sends the party into the represented region. Travel and arrival make it clear that the players are physically inside Wildbound.
3. **Read the real place:** landmarks, creatures, local people, and event verses reveal what the board got right, wrong, or left out.
4. **Survive and make progress:** complete event conditions, fight when needed, explore, gather supplies, help locals, and find the next safe route. Each biome uses distinct threats and traversal.
5. **Find the next crossing:** recover a route clue, restore a Waystone, or reach a return space. The board remains a tangible objective inside the fiction.
6. **Return and debrief:** arrive back at the house or board clearing, see what the event changed, keep earned discoveries, and choose whether to play another round.

### Long loop: learn the board, learn the world

Across sessions, players add event cards/verses, route marks, tokens, creatures, and notes to a **campaign board**. This record shows where the party has been, which events they survived, and which rules they understand. In Wildbound, opened routes and saved landmarks become a navigable campaign. In Game Night, acquired tools and knowledge can make later house events more manageable without removing uncertainty.

Progression should expand strategies and story access, not make the party so powerful that events stop feeling dangerous. Rewards can include protective gear, new tools, a companion, a clue that reveals a rule, a shortcut between biomes, or a new way to complete a category of event. Home customization gives the group a personal place to display its campaign history.

## Event design: make each roll tell a small story

Every event should have a short design card with:

- **The verse:** evocative clue with a memorable image or sound.
- **The arrival:** exactly what enters or changes, and how the players recognize it.
- **The threat:** what can go wrong and who/what is endangered.
- **The rule:** what must be done to resolve it; this should be discoverable, not arbitrary.
- **The co-op opportunities:** at least two useful jobs or tactics so players can help in different ways.
- **The ending:** what changes when the players succeed or fail.
- **The mode mapping:** how the same event works in the house and inside Wildbound.

Example: **“When the red moon crosses the hollow, keep three lanterns burning.”** In the house, a cold wind moves room-to-room and a shadow creature extinguishes lamps; players defend the board room and relight lamps around the house. In the Shadow Realm, the same verse is a real route trial: the group must carry light between three standing stones while hounds press from beyond the veil. In both versions the rule is clear, teamwork matters, and the event is recognizably the same.

## Cast and factions: roles for future story planning

Keep named characters tied to places and motives. The following are role concepts, not decisions to replace current NPCs:

- **The Keeper:** a present-day custodian of lost things, rules, and travelers. They may know how to read the board but not why it is waking now. Their personal arc can be learning that hiding the board is not the same as understanding it.
- **The Wayfinder:** a Wildbound guide who helps the party read landmarks and old routes. They have practical knowledge, not a complete cosmology lecture.
- **The Boardmaker:** a historical figure or collective whose incomplete records appear as carvings, verses, or margin notes. Their identity can remain contested.
- **The Gatewardens:** local guardians with legitimate reasons to keep crossings closed. They can be adversaries without being evil; the players must earn trust or prove they can handle a route.
- **The Takers:** opportunists who want to exploit the board's access to Wildbound—collecting rare creatures, resources, or artifacts. Their motives can create a clear antagonist thread without explaining every ecological or supernatural problem.
- **The Wildbound inhabitants:** distinct local communities, travelers, and creatures with their own stakes. Avoid making every biome a themed monster arena with no ordinary life.

Existing characters such as The Keeper should be preserved and integrated only where the fit is strong. This is a story proposal, not a retcon.

## How this stands apart

- The board game is not a menu or framing metaphor; it is the literal cause of gameplay events.
- The party can experience the same supernatural premise in two scales: defending the place where they play, or being pulled into the world represented on the board.
- Event verses and rules connect narrative, objective, and co-op behavior. Players can learn how to outsmart the game.
- The house makes ordinary objects and spaces part of the adventure, giving domestic co-op its own identity.
- Wildbound is a coherent world with history and local inhabitants; its biomes are not interchangeable stages generated only to host combat.
- The campaign artifact—the board—changes with the group's discoveries and becomes a record of their play together.
- The tone can switch from funny chaos to genuine peril without changing premise: the board is playful in presentation, but the consequences are real.

## Tone and presentation

Aim for **adventurous wonder, playful group energy, and clear peril**. Think friends trying to follow confusing rules while the living room turns into a jungle, then realizing that the jungle has people and history of its own. Humor comes from the collision between ordinary home life and impossible events, not from making every threat harmless.

Use short verses, board illustrations, token animations, sound cues, and environmental changes to deliver story. Keep dialogue optional and readable. Let players understand the immediate danger quickly; reserve deeper history for clues they can choose to pursue.

## Planning sequence before implementation

1. **Confirm the premise and modes:** board events become real; Game Night and Wildbound mode are distinct but connected.
2. **Set the canon roster:** decide whether the intended world has eight or nine non-house destinations, how Temple/Jungle is counted, and whether Urban is a house setting or a future biome.
3. **Define the board's origin and rules:** choose how much of the proposed Waystone/boundary-tree history to adopt; write what rolls, verses, tokens, failure, and return mean in fiction.
4. **Write the opening session:** house discovery, first harmless proof, first dangerous event, transition into Wildbound, first return goal.
5. **Build one event in both modes on paper:** use the red-moon lantern example or a selected existing encounter and confirm it is fun, clear, and distinct in each setting.
6. **Outline each region:** one defining local truth, one local group or character, three event concepts, one major mystery, and how the board route connects onward.
7. **Only after concept review, turn the approved outline into implementation slices.**

## Decisions for the next lore pass

- Is the board's origin a handmade map from Wildbound, a game based on an older artifact, or an object whose creator is unknown?
- Is the house explicitly our world, a stylized shared “game night” space, or the player's chosen home?
- Should the group defend the house from events as a full mode, a campaign prologue, or both?
- Does Wildbound mode begin by pulling the whole group in, or can events pull individual players/creatures across at different times?
- Is the intended world roster eight non-house destinations? The current source list contains nine; confirm whether one is out of scope, grouped, or newly added to canon.
- Is “urban” meant to describe the real-world house/streets, or a separate city biome inside Wildbound?
- How scary should the event escalation become, especially for younger players and couch co-op groups?

## Success criteria for the pitch

After a short session, a player should be able to say: “We played a board game, it made the event happen, and we had to work together to finish its rule.” After entering Wildbound, they should also be able to say what makes that world different from a random fantasy map. Both modes should feel like complete versions of the same promise, and a later visit should make the board or world visibly remember what the party did.
